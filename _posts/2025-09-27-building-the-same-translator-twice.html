Project writeup: NLP / Neural Machine Translation

Building the same translator twice: What the LSTM taught me before I dropped it

A hands-on exploration of sequence-to-sequence translation: rebuilding the foundations with an LSTM, then replacing it with a Transformer to understand exactly what changed and why.

Starting with the papers, not the code

Two papers were on my mind before I wrote a line of code. Sutskever, Vinyals and Le's "Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks" (2014, arXiv:1409.3215) is the paper that made seq2seq a standard approach in the first place: encoder-decoder LSTMs mapping one sequence to another.

The other was Luong, Pham and Manning's "Effective Approaches to Attention-based Neural Machine Translation" (2015, arXiv:1508.04025), which is where the attention mechanism I used actually comes from.

Reading both before building anything gave me a clearer sense of what problem attention was solving, and why it mattered enough to bother implementing.

Why build something I knew I would replace?

I started this project with an LSTM, not because I thought it was the best tool for the job, but because I wanted to actually understand what was happening inside a seq2seq model before letting a pretrained Transformer do the work for me.

That decision turned out to be worth it, even though the LSTM itself never produced anything I'd call good.

The LSTM experiments

The first version was bare bones: a seq2seq LSTM trained on 10–20k sentence pairs, just enough to validate that the pipeline worked end to end.

Then I added embedding layers, testing dimensions of 50, 100, and 200, to see how much semantic representation actually mattered at that scale.

After that I went back and commented every line, mostly for my own benefit, so I could trace exactly what each part of the model was doing on a second pass.

That step alone taught me more than the training runs did.

Where attention changed things

The real turning point was adding Luong attention. Results improved noticeably with a 256 latent dimension and a 10k line input size, which was useful to see in practice rather than just reading about it in a paper.

I also swapped out regex-based tokenisation for Hugging Face's BPE tokeniser. That removed the need to hand-build input and target dictionaries, but it came with a tradeoff: smaller tokens actually hurt translation quality until I tuned the parameters properly.

Early stopping and a dynamic learning rate helped tighten up training time and output quality after that.

When the model worked, but was not useful

By the end of the LSTM phase, the model worked in the sense that it produced grammatically plausible French. But it was slow to train, sensitive to almost every hyperparameter, and the translations themselves were rough.

Understandable, but not something you'd trust for anything real.

That was expected. Recurrent architectures trained from scratch on a modest dataset are never going to compete with something that has already seen millions of sentence pairs.

Rebuilding with a Transformer

So I rebuilt the whole pipeline around a Transformer architecture instead, using a pretrained Helsinki-NLP model and fine-tuning it with Hugging Face's Seq2SeqTrainer rather than hand-rolling the training loop.

The shift was almost immediate. Dataset cleaning and preprocessing, automatic tokenisation, and inference all became more standardised and less fragile.

The outputs were noticeably more fluent right out of the gate, even before any serious fine-tuning had happened.

The easy part was the interface

Wrapping inference in Streamlit was the easy part, and it shows: a text area, a button, and a single call out to the translate function from inference.py.

No real UI logic to speak of. That is exactly the point. All the actual work happened in getting the model itself to produce something worth displaying.

What I actually learned

The LSTM wasn't wasted effort even though I abandoned it.

Watching where it struggled — tokenisation choices, attention mechanisms, latent dimension size — gave me a much clearer sense of what the Transformer was doing better and why.

This project was an exercise in understanding the foundations of modern NLP systems: not just using a Transformer, but understanding the problems it was designed to solve. If I'd started with the pretrained model straight away, I don't think I'd have appreciated the improvement anywhere near as much.
