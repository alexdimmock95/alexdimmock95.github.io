Project writeup: woodwork / joinery

Studying Dovetails: Precision, Repetition, Perfection

Second project at the Building Crafts College: a box for sheet music and trinkets. Point of it is practice — cutting loads of dovetails until precision, repetition and consistency are actually in my hands, not just something I understand in theory.

First attempt was a practice box in cherry. Dovetails are pin and tail joints, usually cut at a 1:7 or 1:8 angle. But the angle isn't the important bit — what matters is that each tail matches its pin exactly, whatever angle you use. Cherry's straight grain doesn't hide mistakes, so it was a good wood to learn on.

Next was sapele, a tropical hardwood that smells like cinnamon when you plane it. Interlocking grain made it easier to work than cherry — cuts and planing felt more even, without the grain dragging the tool like it does with straight-grained wood.

Even a careful joint can have small gaps after glue-up. Fix: chisel off a paper-thin bit of waste sapele, tap it into the gap, pare off the excess. Same grain direction as the tail, so it disappears — you can't tell it's two pieces. Small trick, but it's the difference between okay joinery and good joinery.

Base and lid

The base is sapele too — glued up, run through a thickness planer to get it flat and to size, then slotted into a groove cut on a router table.

Lid has a 4x4mm square routed through one piece, with a 4mm brass rod through it. The rod shows on both outer sides of the box, like an axle running through the lid.
